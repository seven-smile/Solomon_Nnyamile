RPA & Computer Vision Automation

Client

Inngen Ghana Technologies

Role

RPA Developer

Duration

Nov 2023 – Mar 2025

The Problem

Inngen Ghana was dealing with two separate but related pain points. First, their recruitment process involved manually reviewing and sorting large volumes of CVs against job specifications -a slow, inconsistent, and frankly boring task that was eating hours of staff time and producing variable results depending on who was doing the screening that day.

Second, they had a solution to process and analyse visual content -images and videos -for security and operational purposes. This is a manual process that required human analysts to review footage frame by frame, which was not scalable given the volume of content they deal with.

Both cases had the same root cause: humans doing repetitive pattern-matching work that software could do faster and more consistently.

The CV Classification Bot

I designed and built a UiPath automation solution that reads incoming CVs, extracts relevant fields -education, skills, experience, years in role -and classifies them against a defined job specification. The bot sorts candidates into tiers based on match quality, producing a ranked output that the HR team could actually use, rather than a pile of PDFs to read through from scratch.

CVs are not standardised documents. People format them differently, use different section headings, include different fields. A robust extraction engine has to handle that variability without falling apart when it encounters something unexpected. I spent considerable time on the extraction logic -building fallbacks, testing against diverse CV formats, and tuning the classification rules so they reflected what actually made a candidate strong for a given role rather than just keyword-matching.

The result was a system that could do in minutes what previously took a full afternoon of manual review.

Computer Vision for Acts of Violence Detection

The second workstream was a different kind of problem. Using computer vision, I built an automation pipeline that could analyse image and video content to detect potential indicators of criminal activity. This involved integrating trained visual models into a UiPath workflow so the detection could run as part of a broader automated process rather than requiring a human analyst to review footage frame by frame.

The ethical dimension of this work isn't something to be taken lightly. Automated detection systems in security contexts carry real risk if they produce false positives or are deployed without appropriate human oversight. I built the system to flag, not to act -the output was always a prioritised queue for human review, never an autonomous decision. That distinction matters.

Outcome

The numbers tell the story clearly. Before the bot, screening 150 CVs took a team the better part of a week -reading, comparing, sorting manually against a job spec. After, the same 150 CVs go through initial screening in just over an hour or two. The hours that were going into mechanical CV sorting are now available for the work that actually requires human judgement -interviews, assessment, decision-making.

The consistency improvement is harder to quantify but equally real. Manual screening varies depending on who's doing it and how tired they are by CV number 80. The bot applies the same criteria to every document, every time. That kind of consistency matters when you're making consequential decisions about people's careers.

The computer vision pipeline gave the team a scalable way to process visual content that previously had no automated solution at all. The workflow became part of their standard operational toolkit.

This engagement also led me to pursue and obtain the UiPath Certified Professional -Automation Developer certification, formalising the skills I had been applying in practice.
